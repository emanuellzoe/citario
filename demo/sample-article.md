# Shade, Soil Moisture and Yield in Smallholder Coffee Plots of West Java

Sari Wulandari (fictional author for the Citario demo). This paper and every number in it are invented for a software demo.

## 1. Abstract

Smallholder coffee farmers are told that shade trees protect yields during dry seasons. Evidence from Indonesian plots is thin and mostly anecdotal. We measured soil moisture and cherry yield across shaded and unshaded plots over two harvests. Shaded plots kept more soil moisture in the driest month and lost less yield in the drought year. The effect was smaller than extension materials usually claim.

## 2. Introduction

Coffee is a cash crop for roughly two million Indonesian households. Most of them farm less than one hectare. Extension services have promoted shade-grown coffee for two decades. The claim rests on studies from Central America with different rainfall patterns. Farmers in West Java often remove shade trees to plant more coffee bushes per hectare. Whether that choice costs them yield in dry years is an open question for this region.

## 3. Related Work

Earlier field trials in Costa Rica reported that moderate shade reduced yield in wet years but stabilised it in dry ones. A meta-analysis of agroforestry systems found large variation between sites and warned against general rules. Work in Sumatra measured shade and biodiversity but did not record yield. Remote-sensing studies estimated canopy cover at scale but could not observe soil moisture under the canopy. None of these studies covered smallholder plots on the volcanic soils of West Java.

## 4. Methods

We recruited farmers through two cooperatives in Garut and Bandung regencies. The final sample was 184 plots on 61 farms, of which 97 plots had more than 30 percent canopy shade and 87 had less than 10 percent. Plots with between 10 and 30 percent shade were excluded to keep the comparison clear. Each plot received a capacitive soil moisture sensor at 20 centimetres depth, logging every hour. Canopy shade was measured with hemispherical photographs taken at the start of each season. Cherry yield was weighed at the cooperative scale and recorded per plot for the 2024 and 2025 harvests. The 2025 season was a drought year, with rainfall 38 percent below the ten-year mean in August. We compared groups with a mixed-effects model that included farm as a random effect and elevation, bush age and fertiliser use as covariates. Analysts were blind to the shade group when cleaning the yield data.

## 5. Results

In the driest month of 2025, mean soil moisture was 24.1 percent in shaded plots and 17.6 percent in unshaded plots. In the normal year, shaded plots yielded 6 percent less cherry per bush than unshaded plots. In the drought year, shaded plots lost 11 percent of their normal-year yield while unshaded plots lost 27 percent. Across both years combined, the yield difference between groups was not statistically significant. The protective effect in the drought year held after controlling for elevation and bush age. Fertiliser use did not change the direction of any result.

## 6. Discussion

Shade did not raise average yield, but it reduced the loss in a bad year. For a household that depends on one harvest, lower variance may matter more than a higher mean. The effect was about half of what extension leaflets in the region describe. Our sample covers two regencies and two harvests, so the result should not be generalised to all of Java. A longer panel and a cost estimate for shade-tree management would make the recommendation actionable.
